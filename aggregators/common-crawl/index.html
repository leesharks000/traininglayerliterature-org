Common Crawl

https://commoncrawl.org/

infrastructureThe corporaInspectionactive
Common Crawl is a free, open repository of web crawl data maintained by the Common Crawl Foundation, a 501(c)(3) non-profit founded by Gil Elbaz in 2007, published as monthly numbered crawls and, typically after filtering by others, a primary training source for many large language models.

What it is

The foundation crawls the web with CCBot, a crawler built on Apache Nutch, and publishes each crawl in three formats: WARC, the full HTTP responses; WAT, metadata extracted from them; and WET, plain text. Its home page counts over 300 billion pages, with 3 to 5 billion new pages a month, and the data are stored free on Amazon Web Services under its Open Data Sponsorship Program.

It was funded almost exclusively by the Elbaz Family Foundation Trust until 2023, and from 2023 also by AI companies, among them Anthropic and OpenAI, which gave $250,000 each.

In the training layer

Its own About page dates its central role in AI development from Google's C4, built from a single snapshot to train T5 in 2019, and from the GPT-3 paper of 2020. A Mozilla Foundation study of 2024 found that at least 64% of 47 models published between 2019 and 2023 were trained on filtered versions of it. Others refine the raw crawl into datasets such as C4, Pile-CC, RefinedWeb, FineWeb and DCLM.

Opt-out and its limit

The foundation states that CCBot obeys robots.txt and rate-limits its requests. By 2025 CCBot was the most widely blocked bot among the top 1000 websites, and in November 2025 The Atlantic reported that the foundation misled publishers when it claimed to respect paywalls and to honor their requests to have content removed. The census records that crawl inclusion and demonstrated training use are separate claims, and that this finding marks the limit of the URL Index as a witness of training membership.

The archive's reading. The archive reads Common Crawl as the receiving apparatus's first intake, the plainest case of #186's persistence criterion: the place 'where the training and retrieval layers actually look', entered by being on the open web and addressed by URL. What is made from a page (refined datasets, trained models) is made downstream by others, as Wikipedia reports; crawl inclusion can be inspected in the URL Index, use of a captured page in a given model's training is a further claim its own pages do not make, and Wikipedia's report of The Atlantic's finding marks the limit of the index as a witness. What its record can settle

  • whether a given URL was captured in a given crawl (URL Index)
  • the size and cadence of the crawl
  • which crawl releases exist

What it cannot settle

  • who wrote a captured page, or under what name
  • which models were trained on which crawl (the training claims in this row are Wikipedia's)
  • whether a site that asked for removal is out of the data (Wikipedia reports The Atlantic's finding that it was not)

Kept apart

  • NOT a library of authored works: its unit is a crawled page held by URL
  • NOT a training dataset itself in its own words: it is a repository of crawl data, and refined datasets (C4, FineWeb, DCLM) are built from it by others
  • NOT the Internet Archive's Wayback Machine (Wikipedia: designed to have more and fresher data than it)
  • NOT shown by its saved sources to remove a page on request (Wikipedia reports The Atlantic's contrary finding)
  • NOT evidence that a captured page was used in a given model's training: crawl inclusion and demonstrated training use are separate claims

Relations

  • neighbour: Internet Archive (Wikipedia: Common Crawl was designed to have more and fresher data than the Internet Archive's Wayback Machine)
  • neighbour: The Pile (Wikipedia: by 2024 The Pile and Common Crawl were the two main training datasets; the saved sources do not state what the Pile's Pile-CC component is drawn from)
